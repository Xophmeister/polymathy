- ## Cue sheet
	- A jar holds bearings of exactly 10.0 g and 11.0 g, and the mean mass per bearing is 10.2 g. What fraction are heavy, and what kind of quantity is 10.2 g – a property of what?
	- Chlorine has a relative atomic mass of 35.5 and two stable isotopes, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37. What are their abundances, and what does the "35" or "37" count?
	- What physically distinguishes a chlorine-35 atom from a chlorine-37 atom, and which count is held fixed between them?
	- Why can't two atoms with different proton counts both be chlorine?
	- Defining an element by proton count looks circular if justified only by the definition. What fact about atoms' behaviour makes proton count the *useful* thing to define elements by, rather than neutron count or mass number?
	- Which part of an atom takes part in bonding, and how does the proton count reach it?
	- CHEM1.2 / CHEM1.3: Neutral chlorine and neutral argon are both electrically balanced. What must therefore distinguish their reactivity?
	- Why do the nuclear forces play no part in chemistry? Give the relevant length scales.
	- If isotopes are chemically interchangeable, why can deuterium-containing compounds react measurably more slowly than their hydrogen counterparts? What is the one property the extra neutron changes?
	- PHYS1.11: Two molecules at the same temperature differ in mass by a factor of two. What is shared between them, and what is the ratio of their speeds? Derive it.
	- CHEM1.11 / PHYS1.17: Translational speed alone predicts a deuterium rate effect of about 1.4, yet observed effects reach 6–7. Where does the extra come from, and why does an exponential matter?
	- Why are kinetic isotope effects essentially a hydrogen phenomenon?
	- State the numbers of protons, neutrons and electrons in a neutral atom of sodium-23, and in Na⁺. Why is the ion formed by removing electrons rather than adding protons?
	- Boron has a relative atomic mass of 10.81, with isotopes boron-10 and boron-11. What percentage is boron-11?
	- CHEM1.9: You weigh out equal masses of ordinary boron and isotopically pure boron-11. What differs between the two samples, by roughly how much, and why does it matter for a reaction?
- ## Error cues
	- It's sometimes said that an element becomes reactive when its electrons aren't balanced by its protons, so that a "spare" or "missing" electron drives bonding. What's wrong with that?
	- One argument for why an element must have a fixed proton count runs: if two atoms in a sample had different proton counts, the unbalanced positive charge would make them repel each other. What's wrong with that argument?
	- Heavy water, D₂O, reacts differently from H₂O. One explanation offered is that, being a compound rather than a single element, the molecule's overall electron arrangement differs, and that is where the difference lies. What's wrong with that?
	- Two gases sit at the same temperature, one with molecules twice as massive as the other. Someone concludes the heavier molecules move at a quarter of the speed. What's wrong with that?
	- Substituting deuterium for hydrogen can slow a reaction roughly fivefold. Someone reasons that substituting boron-11 for boron-10 in a boron reaction should therefore slow it by a comparable factor. What's wrong with that reasoning?
- ## Summary
	- Explain to a sceptic who says: "Isotopes are a fudge. The periodic table is supposed to list pure substances with definite masses, then you admit chlorine is a mixture with mass 35.5 – a mass no chlorine atom has. Either the table is wrong or isotopes were invented to patch it."
